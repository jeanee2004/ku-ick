A Korean bank account isn't optional here — it's how you pay rent, get your phone bill auto-debited, receive a part-time paycheck or scholarship, and split the bill at dinner the way everyone else does. The good news: nearly every major bank supports foreign account holders, and if you're 19 or older, you qualify.

**Quick answer:** You can open a basic Korean bank account with just your passport, but full mobile banking, a debit card, and normal transfer limits require your Residence Card. If you can wait until your Residence Card arrives, do — it saves you from opening an account twice.

## What You'll Need

- Passport
- Residence Card (if you have it — see below for what changes if you don't yet)
- Proof of address (a lease agreement or a utility bill)
- A taxpayer number from your home country, if your bank asks for one (e.g., a US SSN/ITIN, Japan's My Number, or a Chinese ID/passport number)
- If you're a student: your enrollment certificate or admission letter
- If you'll be paid a salary: an employment certificate or contract, and proof of the upcoming deposit

## Option A — Opening With Your Residence Card (Full Service)

This gets you the full package: mobile banking, a standard account, and a debit card.

- Visit a branch with your documents — mornings are quieter, and a branch inside your university is usually the easiest option.
- A teller walks you through the whole setup in person.
- Ask specifically for a **debit card with built-in transit card functionality** — it doubles as your T-money card, which is genuinely convenient.
- While you're at the branch, install the bank's app and finish setting up your digital certificate (공동/금융인증서) with the teller right there, rather than fighting with it alone later.

If you already have both your Residence Card and a Korean phone number, some banks let you skip the branch entirely and open the account directly through their mobile app.

## Option B — Opening Before Your Residence Card Arrives

You can still open something with just your passport — but it's limited.

- Visit a branch and consult with a teller, same as above.
- A passport-only account is essentially cash storage: no card is issued, and you can only deposit or withdraw cash at an ATM.
- Once your Residence Card arrives, go back to convert it into a full account. Bring the card itself, along with a certificate of alien registration from your district office (주민센터).

## Tips & Precautions

- **Go where the help is set up for foreigners.** The Seoul Global Center or a local foreign workers' support center can point you to branches with dedicated foreigner service counters, which makes the whole process smoother.
- **If you're a university student, use your school's partner bank.** Most universities have an on-campus bank tied to student ID cards — you can combine your student ID and debit card into one, and it makes paying tuition and campus fees far easier.
- **Expect a transaction limit account at first.** New accounts are opened with capped transfer limits as a fraud-prevention measure — typically around 1,000,000 KRW a day through mobile/ATM and 3,000,000 KRW at a branch counter.
- **Lifting that limit is straightforward.** Show a tuition payment receipt, three to six months of steady automatic bill payments (phone, utilities), or part-time salary deposits, and a branch visit can get the limit lifted.
- **Never lend out your account.** Voice phishing scams specifically target students who aren't fully comfortable in Korean. Never hand over your bankbook, card, or password to anyone — this is one of the most common ways international students get pulled into fraud without realizing it.

## The Bottom Line

The cleanest path is simple: get your Residence Card first, then head to your university's partner bank branch and open everything — account, debit card, mobile banking — in one visit. If you truly can't wait, a passport-only cash account works as a stopgap, but plan to convert it the moment your card arrives.
